## What "Net Carbs" Actually Means on a Tortilla Label

This is where most people get tripped up, and honestly, I got it wrong myself the first time I evaluated these products. The net carb math sounds simple: [total carbs](/net-carbs-vs-total-carbs/) minus fiber, minus sugar alcohols. But not all fiber is created equal.

Soluble corn fiber, which is what Mission uses in their Carb Balance line, does raise [blood sugar](/keto-could-unlock-exercise-benefits-blocked-by-high-blood-sugar/) in some people. Not as much as regular starch, but it's not a free pass either. A 2020 study published in the *Journal of the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics* found that soluble corn fiber had a glycemic index of roughly 35, compared to 70 for white bread. So it matters, but it's not zero. If you're using continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) or tracking ketones closely, test your personal response instead of assuming the label math is your math.

Chicory root fiber and psyllium husk, used by some brands, behave differently again. In my experience working with clients who test their ketones, psyllium-based products cause fewer glucose spikes than corn fiber products, though the sample size I can speak to confidently is probably around 30 people. The research on individual variation here is genuinely not settled.

**Mission Carb Balance** is the one I recommend most often because it's everywhere. Walmart, Target, most grocery chains. It behaves almost exactly like a flour tortilla in a pan, and if you warm it over direct flame for 20 seconds per side it gets that slight char that makes it feel real. The texture on day two isn't great if you refrigerate them, but that's fixable: 15 seconds in a dry skillet brings them back.

**La Banderita Carb Counter** is my current value pick as of August 2026. Same concept as Mission but slightly lower net carbs per tortilla and consistently $4.50 or under at most H-E-B and Walmart locations I've checked. They don't roll quite as well cold, so warm them first or they crack at the fold.

**Siete Almond Flour tortillas** are good. Genuinely good. But 8g net carbs is borderline for strict keto, and the tapioca starch is the reason for that. Some clients use Siete successfully in maintenance or low-carb (not strict keto) phases. I wouldn't use them as a daily driver if your ceiling is 20g net carbs per day.

The **Crepini Egg White Wraps** deserve their own paragraph. One net carb, no fiber tricks, genuinely just egg white formed into a thin sheet. They taste like an egg white. They feel like an egg white. They're not a tortilla. I've seen people love them for breakfast wraps specifically, and I've seen people spit them into the sink. Know what you're signing up for.

## Homemade: Worth It or Not?

Honestly? For most people, no. The store brands have gotten good enough that making your own is a weekend project, not a weekly routine.

That said, if you have specific sensitivities to the additives in commercial brands (methylcellulose in some of them, carrageenan in others), or if you genuinely enjoy cooking, a homemade [almond flour](/how-to-make-keto-bread-without-almond-flour/) tortilla is better than everything on that list above in terms of texture and ingredient control. The recipe I've used the most is simple: 1.5 cups fine almond flour, 1 egg, 1 tbsp water, a pinch of salt, and if you want pliability, half a teaspoon of xanthan gum. Press them between parchment on a tortilla press (I use a 10" cast iron one I got for $22 on Amazon), cook in a dry skillet on medium-high about 90 seconds per side.

What the recipes never tell you: the dough needs to rest for 5 minutes after mixing or it tears on the press. I ruined a dozen before I figured that out.

Scenario walkthrough: Dana, the client I mentioned at the top, switched from Mission Carb Balance to homemade almond flour tortillas for weekends and kept Mission for weekdays. Result: she dropped from roughly 15g net carbs per day to 11g without changing anything else in her diet, and she stopped feeling deprived entirely. The mental shift of having a real "taco night" once a week was, in her words, what made keto sustainable.

## The Honestly-Not-Worth-It Products

A few things I'd skip. Coconut flour tortillas, in my experience, are usually chalky and stiff unless they're fresh and warm. Once they cool, they become structural. Cassava flour tortillas (Siete makes these too) are actually delicious but run 22-26g net carbs per tortilla, which makes them flatly incompatible with strict keto regardless of how the packaging is positioned. Check the label before assuming something in the "keto" section is actually keto.

Also, any tortilla marketed specifically as "zero carb" that lists chicory root fiber as the first ingredient deserves a skeptical second look. Chicory root (inulin) is fermentable fiber and generally fine, but a few people experience meaningful GI distress with large amounts. If you've had issues with keto products causing bloating, that's often the culprit.
