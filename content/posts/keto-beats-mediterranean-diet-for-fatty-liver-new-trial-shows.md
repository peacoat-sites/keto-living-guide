A new clinical trial is making it harder to dismiss ketogenic diets as a niche fad for weight-loss enthusiasts. Published November 20, 2026 in *Cell Metabolism*, the Washington University School of Medicine study put three popular eating patterns head-to-head in adults with obesity, prediabetes, and fatty liver disease. The result was not subtle: keto reduced liver fat by an average of 67% over roughly four to five months, compared to 45% in both the Mediterranean and low-fat diet groups. The kicker isn't the absolute numbers, it's that all three groups lost similar amounts of weight. Same scale movement, very different liver outcomes.

That gap matters because MASLD (metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease, formerly called NAFLD) is estimated to affect roughly one in four adults worldwide, making it one of the most common chronic liver conditions. That's not a niche condition. For years, the clinical advice has been "lose weight and move more," with the assumption that the specific diet is secondary. This trial puts that assumption under real pressure.

The trial was small, 42 participants randomized across three arms, so it isn't the final word. But it was rigorously controlled, the diets were supervised, and the liver-fat measurements came from MRI imaging rather than self-reported proxies. This is about as clean as a dietary intervention study gets.

## What the Numbers Actually Show

The insulin data is where the story gets interesting. Blood insulin levels fell 74% in the keto group, 44% in the Mediterranean group, and only 27% in the low-fat group. Chronically elevated insulin is one of the main drivers of hepatic fat accumulation, so a sharper insulin reduction likely explains much of the extra liver benefit. This isn't a new hypothesis; researchers have proposed it for years. What's new is a controlled head-to-head trial actually showing the effect at scale.

The prediabetes reversal numbers are equally striking. Half of the keto participants no longer met the clinical criteria for prediabetes after four to five months, versus 29% on Mediterranean and just 7% on the low-fat diet. Seven percent. If a drug produced that outcome against a competitor, it would be considered an indictment of the losing drug.

Here's a side-by-side view of the three diets on the main outcomes:

| Outcome | Ketogenic | Mediterranean | Low-Fat |
|---|---|---|---|
| Liver fat reduction | 67% | 45% | 45% |
| Prediabetes reversal | 50% | 29% | 7% |
| Insulin reduction | 74% | 44% | 27% |
| Cholesterol increase | None | Not reported | Not reported |

## Why Diet Composition, Not Just Calories, Seems to Matter Here

The "a calorie is a calorie" argument has always been a bit of an oversimplification, and fatty liver disease is one of the clearest places to see why. The liver processes dietary carbohydrates into fat through a process called de novo lipogenesis. When you restrict carbohydrates sharply enough to push the body into ketosis, that pathway quiets down significantly. At the same time, ketones themselves may have direct anti-inflammatory effects on liver tissue.

The Washington University findings align with earlier mechanistic work, but prior trials often confounded diet composition with calorie restriction or [weight loss](/keto-for-weight-loss/) differences. This trial's ability to show liver-specific benefits at similar weight loss makes the composition argument much harder to dismiss.

That said, 42 participants over five months is not enough to settle the debate. We don't yet know whether the liver-fat advantage holds at 12 or 24 months, whether it applies equally to people without prediabetes, or whether the benefits would differ in populations with more advanced liver fibrosis.

## What This Means If You Have Fatty Liver Disease

If you've been told you have MASLD (or its older name, NAFLD), and you're trying to decide whether diet type matters beyond just losing weight, this trial gives you a more specific answer than most prior research: yes, carbohydrate restriction appears to produce faster and larger liver-fat reduction than a low-fat or even Mediterranean approach, at least in the short term.

That doesn't mean keto is easy or right for everyone. The dropout rates in supervised dietary trials tend to be lower than in real-world practice. The diet requires consistent carbohydrate restriction to below roughly 20 to 50 grams of total carbs per day to maintain ketosis, and that's a significant lifestyle shift. If you're considering trying it and need a starting point, a [keto macro calculator](/keto-macro-calculator/) can give you specific daily fat, protein, and carb targets based on your size and goals, which removes some of the guesswork early on.

Work with a physician or qualified healthcare provider before making dietary changes if you have an existing liver condition, diabetes, or take medications affected by carbohydrate intake. Diet composition can influence blood sugar and medication dosing in meaningful ways.

## Where Keto Still Gets Oversold

Nothing here suggests keto is universally superior or that everyone with a liver should adopt it immediately. A few honest caveats:

The Mediterranean diet produced a 45% liver fat reduction, which is not trivial. For someone who finds keto unsustainable, the Mediterranean pattern still delivers substantial metabolic benefit. Adherence over years matters more than optimal outcomes over five months.

The cholesterol finding, that the keto group saw no increase despite getting 73% of calories from fat, is reassuring but was measured over a short window. Longer trials show mixed results depending on the specific types of fat consumed and the individual's lipid response profile. Some people see significant LDL increases on high-fat diets, particularly with high saturated fat intake. That's worth monitoring.

And keto is not a single diet. A [keto diet](/what-is-keto-diet/) built around olive oil, avocado, nuts, fatty fish, and non-starchy vegetables is metabolically different from one built around bacon, butter, and processed keto snack bars. The trial didn't specify which fat sources participants used, and that detail matters for long-term cardiovascular outcomes.

The Washington University trial gives liver patients, clinicians, and anyone with prediabetes the clearest diet-comparison evidence to date. It won't be the last word, but it's a meaningfully better data point than what we had before.
